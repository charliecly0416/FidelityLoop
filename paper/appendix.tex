\section{Reproduction and claim--evidence map}
The source release includes synthetic interface checks, historical facade
compatibility checks, and a standalone same-runtime accounting verifier.
\path{REPRODUCING.md} gives the commands and expected outputs. In the same-runtime comparison package,
\texttt{python -B verify.py} checks the manifest and recomputes 169 resource/cost

\texttt{python -B -m unittest}; physical-run acceptance is inherited.

The paired trace corpus\footnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/FidelityLoop-144B/}}
covers the held-out, mixed-policy, deployment, screening, engine, arrival,
and 14B studies:
197 technically accepted physical runs (including 41 strict P1+ failures)
and 239 unique frozen predictions form 555 model/run pairs. Predictions are
reused across physical repeats. The index separately lists eight excluded
technical or aborted attempts; it does not provide full traces for them.
Each released record contains aligned request timing and terminal status,
control targets, lifecycle events, and resource/cost primitives, with prompts
and machine identifiers removed. The standard-library loader and examples
recompute feasibility-direction agreement and scenario-cost MAE.
The worked screening example also returns mismatched request IDs and reports
false accepts/rejects with their physical denominators;
\texttt{python -B verify.py} checks the complete corpus after extraction.
This release supports analysis of the recorded deployments, not unseen-workload
validation or rerunning model inference. The later 14B-30, Azure, and Vidur-backed retrospective predictions are
available in the anonymous repository and are not included in these counts. The corpus's \path{SCHEMA.md} specifies the projection
and accounting boundaries.
The full-day and A800 capacity studies below report eight and 36 additional
physical executions, respectively, outside the historical corpus counts above.
Section~\ref{sec:realapi} adds 36 prospective HTTP executions.
The anonymous repository linked above hosts the evidence and CPU tools;
\path{artifact/} paths in this supplement are relative to its \path{paper/}
directory. Sealed HTTP records are in \path{artifact/realapi_prospective/},
reduced full-day traces and checks in \path{artifact/full_day/}, and capacity
records and verifiers in \path{artifact/capacity_transfer/} and

supplement identify the separately retained full experiment archive. Its

\subsection{External service model in the validation loop}
\label{app:vidur}
The Vidur integration separates service prediction from the outer control
loop. Vidur \citep{vidur2024} supplies its native Sarathi scheduler and token
execution events; FidelityLoop supplies policy observations, tick-based
admission, lifecycle transitions, and deadline/resource accounting. This
allows the same policy and ledger to evaluate an independently profiled
service model.

\paragraph{Service validation.}
New Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct/A100 80GB operator profiles are paired with physical
vLLM 0.9.0 service measurements (FP16, TP=PP=1). Six input/output lengths---
128/128, 256/128, 512/128, 1024/128, 2048/128, and 256/256---at concurrency
one and four, with three repeats, yield 36 groups and 90 requests; two
warmup groups are excluded. Historical prompts supply fixed-length prefixes,
with one repeated prefix in the four-request 2048-token group. Output lengths
are fixed and prefix caching is disabled.

The profile contains 323 MLP and 761 attention measurements from pinned
Sarathi/FlashInfer on PyTorch 2.3; physical service uses vLLM V1/Flash Attention
on PyTorch 2.7. Operator profiling precedes service measurement. CPU prediction
uses 100-tree, depth-eight random forests (seed 42), Sarathi batch cap four
and chunk size 16,384. Inputs contain token budgets and admission offsets,
not completion times. CPU overhead is not modeled and no correction is
fitted to validation latencies.

Request-latency APE is 18.36\% at the median and 19.74\% at P90; group
makespan APE is 18.36\% and 19.66\%. The worst length-by-concurrency median
makespan APE is 19.80\%. These meet the fixed engineering gates of
20\%/35\% median/P90 for both metrics and 50\% worst-condition median.
No condition exceeds the registered 1.5 maximum/minimum repeat-makespan ratio.
All 36 groups underestimate makespan (10.88--26.40\%); the experiment does
not isolate runtime-stack effects from omitted overhead.

\paragraph{Policy and lifecycle evaluation.}
The same profiled service model replaces Local's lookup in 12 CPU rollouts:
all1, all2, H, and H+guard on the original three windows. Each deterministic
prediction is compared with all three existing physical repeats. This is a
retrospective comparison without outcome fitting. Local's startup/shutdown
parameters (33.497/2.224\,s), the guard's original lookup and safety estimates,
and the synthetic API rules stay fixed. Thus lifecycle timing comes from
FidelityLoop's frozen deployment model, not from Vidur's operator profiles.

Both instances begin ready. Completions advance in continuous time; each
one-second tick releases arrivals, evaluates the policy, and fills free local
slots (four per active instance). Draining instances finish accepted work
before shutdown; no new work enters them. Occupation integrates all non-off
states over [0,2100]\,s, excluding setup and cleanup. Unfinished requests
remain in the deadline denominator. Intermediate input lengths and continuous
mixed prefill/decode batches extend beyond the isolated service-screen cases.
All 712,614 native batch queries remain within the measured operator domain,
including 2,340 mixed batches; this domain check does not assert per-shape
service accuracy.

\section{Held-out accounting and gate details}
Completed requests have a completion terminal by the observation cutoff.
On-time completion includes equality with the deadline; late completion is
strictly after it. Unfinished requests lack a completed terminal at cutoff,
and missing or censored requests are not removed from the arrival denominator.
$G_F$ requires every all2 online and offline arrival on time. P1 requires
120/120 offline arrivals on time, at least 99\% of online arrivals on time,
and no unfinished request. P1+ additionally requires every online arrival
on time. These definitions follow \path{scripts/maxopt_v3/acceptance.py};
the extension's strict qualification uses the same timely/unfinished distinction.

\subsection{Guard implementation map}
The frozen \path{scripts/maxopt_v3/policy.py} implements the following
causal projection and state update. The lookup is a conservative estimate, not a
proved upper bound, and infeasible work is still reported rather than guaranteed.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \texttt{validate\_observation} (lines 20--47) accepts only released queues,
device states, state ages, and running-job dispatch times. Future arrivals,
true readiness, and completion times are not policy inputs.
\item \texttt{duration/remaining/delay} (70--90) uses concurrency four. A running
job receives its estimated residual if positive, otherwise a full-service
fallback. Active/draining devices have zero added delay; an off device needs
the frozen safe startup time. A starting device uses the remaining safe-start
interval, then the maximum-start interval (both floored at one second), then
restarts the positive safe-start estimate if both are exceeded. A stopping
device adds remaining shutdown, or a full shutdown fallback, before startup.
\item \texttt{projected} (92--126) chooses the $k$ devices with least lifecycle
delay, with device-ID ties, and builds four slots each. Offline jobs are sorted
by deadline and ID. Without reservation, current online backlog precedes offline
work. With reservation and online backlog, offline jobs are serialized from the
earliest running offline slot, or the earliest slot if none is running. Without
online backlog they use all slots. No future arrivals are projected.
\item \texttt{decide} (128--158) first obtains H's target $b$ and intersects
latched risk IDs with pending offline IDs. It adds IDs with projected finish
$F_j(\max(1,b),r)+m+1\geq d_j$, where $m=5$ s. With active risk and scaling
enabled, it tests $k\in\{1,2\}$ using $F_j(k,r)+m\leq d_j$ for \emph{every}
pending offline job. The target is $\max(b,\min k)$ when feasible; otherwise
it is two and \texttt{infeasible} is set. A raise bypasses cooldown and updates
H's target and last-change time. Safe re-estimation alone does not clear a
latched ID; dispatch or removal from the pending queue does. Reservation is
enabled only while risk is latched and the reservation option is enabled.
\item \texttt{dispatch\_kind} (160--172) never preempts running work. With both
queues nonempty and a reservation active, an already-running offline job leaves
free slots online-first. Otherwise online takes the next slot only when its
head request's estimated slack is at most $m$ and no larger than the offline
head's slack. With no online queue, offline dispatch is unrestricted by the
single-slot reservation; without an active reservation, online has priority.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{API-delay sensitivity in CPU replay}
\label{sec:api-delay-sensitivity}

We replay the four frozen rule policies (all1, all2, H, and H+guard)
on the three original windows under model Local, varying only the fixed
API service delay over $\{1,2,5,10,20,40,60\}$\,s. API capacity remains
eight concurrent requests, with the same 10\,s routing wait, prices,
and policy parameters. All 12 five-second baselines reproduce the
frozen predictions, yielding 84 deterministic CPU scenarios in total.

At the tested delays of 1, 2, 5, and 10\,s, both H+guard and all1
satisfy P1+ in all three windows. Their equal-window cost savings
are 45.07\%, 45.07\%, 45.08\%, and 43.76\%, respectively.
At 20\,s, H+guard misses online deadlines in recovery and steady;
at 40 and 60\,s, online misses occur in all three guarded windows.
Longer API service occupies the finite API slots and changes
subsequent queueing, routing, and GPU control. These results test
sensitivity to the timer assumption; they are CPU predictions,
not additional physical runs or an empirical calibration of a
commercial API.

\section{Full-day arrival and length replay}

\subsection{Inputs and execution contract}
Two complete BurstGPT source days \citep{wang2024burstgpt}, D1 and D2
(origin-aligned day indices 40 and 48),
extend the window studies to continuous execution with variable online lengths.
We apply the existing deterministic hash-thinning rule (retention probability
0.267818), preserving retained timestamps without time compression.
The days are the first eligible complete days in the evaluation region after
excluding the listed physical-evaluation and PPO training/validation windows
with 1,800 s protection on either side. Eligibility requires at least 5,000
retained online requests per day and at most 944 in any half-hour block;
no morphology-class filter or policy prediction enters selection.
These nonconsecutive days are disjoint from those windows; their source rows
had appeared in an earlier full-stream CPU replay.

D1/D2 contain 6,008/5,241 online requests and 5,760 offline jobs each.
Source input/output counts are mapped numerically to Qwen token budgets,
clipped to 3,072 input and 1,024 output tokens. Fixed public-corpus prompts
instantiate these budgets, rather than reconstructing source user content
or its tokenizer. Input/output clipping affects 6/4 requests on D1 and
22/14 on D2. The output cap is frozen by a development microbenchmark:
choose the largest of \{1,024, 768, 512\} whose worst concurrency-four group
time at input length 3,072 is at most 40 s across both GPUs and all three
repeats on the first host; the second host must pass the same cap.

Offline jobs arrive every 15 s with 256 input and 256 output tokens.
Online/offline deadlines remain 60/300 s. The synthetic API retains eight
slots, a 10 s routing wait, and a five-second service timer.
Each run retains controller and lifecycle state for 86,400 s of arrivals
and 300 s of drain; control and dispatch continue throughout the drain.
The 48 half-hour blocks describe one continuous trajectory, not independent
replicates. P1+ requires every planned online and offline request on time,
including undispatched requests and tail obligations. Costs use the main
paper's scenario ledger over the 86,700 s observation interval; setup and
post-cutoff cleanup are separate.

\subsection{Host-local calibration and comparison scope}
Each run uses one dual-A100 80GB host, with Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct FP16 and
the Qwen/vLLM settings in the main experimental setup. The primary D1 and D2
comparisons use hosts M1 and M2, respectively; they are not four-GPU runs.
Each host freezes its own service and lifecycle calibration before its
formal executions. The trace service lookup uses input lengths
\{1,128,512,2048,3072\}, output lengths
\{1,32,128,256,512,768,1024\}, and concurrency one/four, with three repeats
per GPU and an additional 256/256 concurrency-four point.
These measurements instantiate the predictor families for this study;
they are not the unchanged tables of the earlier fixed-length studies.

Lookup-69, Lookup-30, and Local share that host's service table and differ
only in startup: 69.219 s, 30 s, and the measured host median, respectively.
Local's startup medians are 28.486 s on M1 and 29.193 s on M2.
Share retains its analytic service model and 30 s startup.
Median shutdown-to-release latencies are 2.172 s and 2.015 s.
The guard uses one fixed safety profile per host, common to all replay
predictors and physical execution: its startup safety estimates are
34.131 s and 33.361 s, the empirical 95th percentiles of 20 loaded
startup cycles per host. These estimates do not bound every later startup.
No full-day outcome is used to retune calibration, policy thresholds,
or the workload.

The six primary runs compare all2, H, and H+guard
on each day. Two D1 repeats are reported separately. The guarded repeat
uses M1; the H repeat uses M2 and is not paired with the M1 D1 all2 reference
for savings. Primary day and host are coupled, so these observations do not
separate day effects from host effects.

\subsection{Frozen prediction and deployment screening}
Table~\ref{tab:full-day-prediction} compares strict feasibility for the four
primary dynamic runs. All predictors also accept the two physically feasible
all2 anchors. On these four dynamic runs, Share, Lookup-30, and Local each
agree in three cases but falsely accept D1 H+guard; Lookup-69 agrees in one,
falsely accepts both D1 dynamic policies, and falsely rejects D2 H+guard.
These are descriptive case counts, not population error-rate estimates.

The selection rule retains all ties within $10^{-9}$ scenario USD and excludes
the two diagnostic repeats. Share, Lookup-30, and Local select H+guard,
whose two-day feasibility is overturned by physical execution on D1.
Lookup-69 abstains; this is not selection of all2, which is a capacity
reference outside this dynamic candidate pool. Neither dynamic candidate
is physically feasible on both days, so feasible two-day cost regret is
undefined. The D2 conditional saving must not be interpreted as a feasible
two-day deployment result.

A retrospective CPU check replaces only replay startup with five statistics
of the same frozen calibration samples: median, 75th, 90th, 95th percentile,
and maximum. Service, shutdown, and guard parameters remain fixed. Median
through the 95th percentile accept H+guard on both days and reject H;
the sample maxima reject both policies on both days. The maximum-based
predictor therefore correctly abstains for the two-day pool but falsely
rejects the feasible D2 H+guard execution.
All five variants match three of four physical labels, but differ in error
direction and two-day choice.

Full-day validation extends the decision-fidelity test to continuous state
and variable request lengths: physical execution overturns a replay-selected
choice despite timely completion of every online request.
