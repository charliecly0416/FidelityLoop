\section{Introduction}
Elastic LLM serving can share GPU capacity between online requests and
deadline-constrained offline jobs. Releasing instances saves resources, but
delayed startup can jeopardize pending work. Queues, running work, and lifecycle
transitions shape subsequent control decisions. Replay must capture when
capacity becomes available, beyond request timing and total GPU seconds.

FidelityLoop connects policy development and PPO training in replay to
physical validation through a shared policy contract (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}).
It checks predicted deadline feasibility and candidate-policy choices under
the declared cost ledger. In our held-out contrasts, maximum
matched-ID service MAE falls about 35-fold while occupied-resource MAE rises by
64.1\%: closer request timing coexists with worse resource prediction.

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}
\textbf{A reusable closed-loop validation framework.} FidelityLoop supports rule-based policy development and PPO training in replay,
followed by physical validation of frozen policies. Shared
interfaces and request identities align service, control, and resource traces
to check online/offline deadline classifications and candidate-policy choices.
\end{minipage}
\item \textbf{Mechanisms of replay-to-deployment disagreement.} Held-out physical
runs distinguish service and lifecycle (aggregate and trajectory) fidelity.
Event-level analysis and controlled replay contrasts expose startup--cooldown
interactions and cancellation in aggregate resource error.
\item \textbf{Decision-level validation.} Retrospective selection admits failing policies or excludes cheaper feasible
alternatives; prospective selection with generated HTTP responses tests whether
a frozen choice succeeds physically. Physical studies cover
rule-based and frozen PPO policies, full-day arrivals, two serving engines,
a 14B model, quantized execution, and active four-replica control. A guard case study quantifies deadline
protection and resource--latency trade-offs.
\end{enumerate}
We provide paired traces, CPU feasibility/cost tools, and fixed-input Vidur replay\footnote{\href{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/FidelityLoop-144B/}{FidelityLoop anonymous artifact.}}.

\section{Background and motivation}
Mixed online and offline LLM workloads share a limited GPU pool; elastic
scheduling seeks resource savings subject to request deadlines. Physical
evaluation of every candidate is costly. FidelityLoop connects replay-based development and training to physical
validation through common policy interfaces and lifecycle accounting.

Preliminary calibration reduced timing error for matched local requests but
increased occupied-GPU-time error under dynamic scheduling, motivating the
preregistered held-out study.

Startup estimates become decision-sensitive near a controller's cooldown
boundary. In a controlled replay contrast, 69.219 s and 30 s startup estimates
straddle the 60 s cooldown and produce different capacity targets
(Section~\ref{sec:crosslevel}). This motivates checking lifecycle timing through
control actions and deadline outcomes. Figure~\ref{fig:motivation} shows startup
distributions from the held-out physical runs, separating setup from window
scale-out.

\section{Related work}
Simulation already informs deployment: Vidur and Charon guide configuration
search, and InferLine couples profiling and simulation with SLO-aware provisioning
\citep{vidur2024,charon2026,crankshaw2018inferline}.
FidelityLoop adds a validation contract around policy rollouts: each backend
drives decisions from its own causal state, while service and lifecycle
models can change separately. Request-aligned accounting retains undispatched
work and launch-to-release occupation, exposing false accepts/rejects and
the cost of feasible choices. These decision checks complement configuration search.

Cold-start characterization decomposes initialization latency, while
ServerlessLLM reduces startup overhead
\citep{kabakibo2026coldstart,fu2024serverlessllm}. We study how timing errors
interact with controller cooldown to change subsequent actions and deadlines;
controlled replay varies startup with the backend and loading path fixed.

Clockwork targets predictable request timing; vLLM, SGLang, and Sarathi-Serve
optimize execution \citep{gujarati2020clockwork_arxiv,kwon2023vllm,zheng2024sglang,agrawal2024sarathi}.
Provisioning and scheduling connect workload estimates to SLOs
\citep{zhang2019mark,zhang2023shepherd}. ConServe optimizes online/offline
co-serving within an inference engine \citep{qiao2024conserve}; FidelityLoop
validates elastic decisions across replay and physical execution.

Policy design is complementary: Llumnix reschedules LLM requests
\citep{sun2024llumnix}; AAPA predicts serverless scaling needs
\citep{zhang2025aapa}; KIS-S trains PPO autoscaling policies in simulation
for deployment \citep{zhang2025kiss}. We use standard PPO
\citep{schulman2017ppo} to train and freeze policies, then trace incorrect
replay predictions to their physical feasibility and cost consequences.

\section{Problem and metrics}
The primary studies use two accelerators with online and offline deadlines of
60 s and 300 s. Online requests run locally or offload; offline jobs run
locally. Earlier studies use a delay-only synthetic API sink; the prospective
study uses actual HTTP inference. Controlled-window studies contain 120 offline
jobs over 1,800 s of arrivals followed by 300 s of observation. The full-day
study retains these deadlines over 24 h of arrivals and a 300 s drain;
capacity studies use two and four independent replicas under the same deadline contract.
The primary window cost under a fixed USD pricing scenario is
\begin{equation}
 C = .001 G + .002 S + .001 D + 10^{-6} I + 3\!\times\!10^{-6} B + .01 M,
\end{equation}
where $G$ counts GPU seconds from launch to verified release within the window,
$S,D$ are starts and shutdowns, $I,B$ are
synthetic API input and output budgets, and $M$ counts offline deadline misses,
including unfinished requests whose deadlines have passed. Setup and cleanup are reported separately. The prospective study instead
charges all local and provider GPU occupation in $C_{\rm sys}$
(Supplement Section~R); the two ledgers are analyzed separately.

Completed means a recorded completion by the observation cutoff; it may be
\emph{on time} (finish time $\leq$ deadline) or \emph{late} (finish time $>$
deadline). An unfinished request has no completed terminal by the cutoff.
Unfinished or censored work is retained in the arrival denominator. The capacity
gate $G_F$ requires all2 (two GPUs throughout) to complete every online and offline
request on time. P1 requires every offline request on time, at least 99\%
online requests on time, and no unfinished request; P1+ additionally requires
every online request on time. In the original three-window study, P2 is a ratio-of-means saving against all1 (one GPU throughout) computed
within each window and then averaged equally across the three windows.

\emph{Service fidelity} measures request timing error; \emph{lifecycle fidelity}
measures aggregate and time-resolved resource and queue error; \emph{feasibility
fidelity} measures deadline outcomes, including false accepts/rejects.
\emph{Decision fidelity} checks physical feasibility of replay-selected policies
and, for feasible selections, the cost gap to the lowest-cost feasible candidate
in the tested pool. Agreement at one level can coexist with disagreement at another.

\section{Experimental setup}
We evaluate replay fidelity, replay-based policy selection reliability, and
transfer across serving configurations and arrival patterns through common
policy and accounting interfaces.
The preregistered held-out study used two NVIDIA A100 80GB PCIe GPUs at
\experimentfacility{}, each running Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct FP16 with TP1 on
vLLM 0.9.0 \citep{kwon2023vllm}, PyTorch 2.7.0+cu126 and Transformers 4.51.1.
Four local slots per GPU, eager execution, chunked prefill, no prefix caching,
and a dual-ready barrier define the execution contract. Per-run records and
the supplement specify remaining versions, limits, readiness, and release checks.

The deployment-adapted model (Local) was calibrated and frozen before
held-out execution. Legacy-refit is retained as a historical lifecycle
reference; its contrast with Local is descriptive and does not isolate
calibration.

The deployment study executes five frozen PPO checkpoints on two locked
windows (three repeats and two references: 32 runs). Screening adds six
previously undeployed checkpoints (26 runs); a separate mixed-policy cohort
has 38 runs. Checkpoint hashes identify shared policies; each cohort retains
its own recorded lifecycle and hardware conditions.

Transfer studies reuse the policy and accounting contract while varying
serving context or arrivals: SGLang 0.4.8 on dual A100s (26 runs), additional arrivals
on that stack (27), Qwen2.5-14B/vLLM (12), and 7B AWQ-Marlin (12).
Two/four-replica A800 studies add 36 runs with profiles frozen before execution.
The supplement specifies the software, calibration, and repeat matrices.

The prospective study uses two elastic local replicas and two fixed HTTP
provider replicas on four same-host A800s, with the same 7B weights and
decoding settings. Eight existing candidates (H, H+guard, six frozen PPO
checkpoints) run on the two known deployment windows twice, plus four all2
references: 36 cells. Profiles, predictions, and selections are frozen before
any formal setup; no checkpoint is retrained (Supplement Section~R).

\section{Method}
Predictive replay and physical execution share a policy contract.
A policy adapter maps causal queue and device observations to capacity targets;
the guard can also choose dispatch priority. A service predictor supplies
completion timing for admitted work. An execution backend implements launch,
readiness, inference, draining, and verified release. Shared request identities
align service, control, and resource traces; a CPU verifier reconstructs deadline
feasibility and cost from these records. Each one-second tick releases due arrivals, reads backend-visible state,
applies the policy with cooldown/guard, then changes capacity and dispatches.
Completion, readiness, and release retain their backend timestamps; only
ready, accepting devices admit work. Replay contrasts vary timing models
while preserving these interfaces and accounting semantics.
By default, ready local slots serve online requests before offline jobs.
Online requests still waiting 10 s after arrival can offload. The earlier
synthetic sink admits four concurrent requests per allocated GPU on a 5 s
timer. In the prospective study, two fixed providers offer eight total slots;
completion requires a validated generated HTTP response, with no timer padding. The guard can prioritize at-risk offline jobs; PPO changes capacity
while retaining the default dispatch and API rules.

The policy and backend adapters have separate interfaces; the supplement
reports their implementation footprint. CPU checks preserve historical
request/event ledgers, and a CPU causal-replay check reproduces all 29,580 logged decisions from
16 same-runtime runs, validating adapter semantics rather than adding an
independent physical cohort. A Vidur service backend
\citep{vidur2024} supplies native scheduling and token execution while
FidelityLoop retains admission, lifecycle transitions, and the frozen guard.
A Qwen2.5-7B/A100 service screen precedes the retrospective policy comparison
in Section~\ref{sec:crosslevel}; the supplement provides profiles and parameters.

For each model prediction and physical repeat of a window, the dimensionless
lifecycle loss is
\begin{equation}
 L=\frac{0.5|\Delta G_{\rm occupied}|+0.25|\Delta G_{\rm active}|}{2T}
   +\frac{0.25|\Delta Q|}{TN},
 \label{eq:lifecycle-loss}
\end{equation}
Here $G_{\rm occupied}$ sums per-GPU time from launch issuance to
verified release, while $G_{\rm active}$ sums time in the active (ready-to-serve) state,
including idle time; both are measured in GPU seconds.
The difference $\Delta$ is prediction minus observation, $T=2100$ s for formal windows,
and $N$ counts \emph{all} online and offline arrivals. $Q$ is the time integral
of the released-but-undispatched queue, in request seconds. Unfinished requests
remain in $N$ and contribute queue time while queued up to cutoff. Two devices
give the $2T$ normalization. We first average losses over repeats within each
window, then weight windows equally; we do not pool requests or restrict $N$ to
matched-local support. $L$ measures prediction error, separately from scenario
cost $C$; the shorter development horizon is stated with that experiment.

\subsection{PPO policy and training protocol}
PPO tests whether replay error also changes learned control behavior, beyond
H's hand-written thresholds. Training uses CPU replay with Lookup-69 or
Lookup-30; physical validation follows checkpoint freezing. At each one-second decision boundary, its 30-dimensional
causal observation contains normalized time and target state, queue counts and
ages/slack, device lifecycle states and ages, running-job summaries, and the API
queue. The capacity action proposes a target of 0, 1, or 2 GPUs; the registered
60 s cooldown filters that proposal before execution, while the offline action
head is fixed to its sole legal value. The reward is the negative incremental
scenario-ledger cost, so training trades occupied resources, lifecycle events,
API use, and deadline misses under the declared contract. The policy observes this causal snapshot of replay state;
future arrivals and physical outcomes are unavailable. We optimize the standard clipped PPO policy surrogate
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 L^{\mathrm{clip}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_t\big[\min\{&r_t(\theta)\widehat A_t,\\
 &\operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat A_t\}\big],
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:ppo-clip}
\end{equation}
where $r_t(\theta)=\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(a_t\mid o_t)$.
The total objective adds the clipped value loss and entropy bonus. The policy
uses a shared two-layer 256-unit ReLU MLP with three capacity logits and a
scalar value head; the offline head is masked to its sole legal action.
Coefficients and optimization settings are recorded in the supplement.

Five frozen checkpoints cover both training environments: a reference and
matched counterpart, two eligible rank-2 checkpoints, and a seed-103 best.
Eligibility requires Lookup-30 P1+ in every validation window without guard;
ordering uses mean cost, checkpoint index, then seed (supplement).
PPO-1/2 were trained with Lookup-69; PPO-3--5 with Lookup-30, using three development
regimes and paired seeds. The supplement records coordinates, inclusion rules,
and a source-row isolation audit of training, validation, and deployment inputs.

\subsection{Controller and guard}
H and its guard separate deadline protection from learned-policy behavior. H uses up-threshold 8, down-threshold 0, and a 60 s cooldown. At each one-second
tick, H reads queue and running counts plus its target/cooldown state, not the
predictor's startup parameter. The guard's lifecycle-delay estimates use separately
frozen safety parameters, distinct from the Lookup-69/Local simulator endpoints.
The guard observes only released queues, device states, and running-job ages;
future arrivals and true completion or readiness times are unavailable. Let $P_t$
be the pending offline request IDs and $b_t$ H's current target after its decision.
The projection $F_j(k,r)$ estimates request $j$'s finish with $k$ devices and
reservation setting $r$, using frozen lifecycle delays, running-job residuals,
and concurrency-four service estimates. It schedules pending jobs by deadline
then request ID over four slots per device. Online backlog precedes offline
work unless reservation ($r=1$) assigns one slot to offline jobs; without online
backlog, offline work can use all slots. The supplement's ``Guard implementation map'' specifies device selection
and overdue-estimate fallbacks. These estimates are not proved completion-time
upper bounds.

For the full guard ($r=1$), margin $m=5$ s and previous risk-ID set $A_{t-1}$,
the primary two-GPU update is
\begin{align}
 R_t &= \{j\in P_t:F_j(\max(1,b_t),1)+m+1\geq d_j\},\nonumber\\
 A_t &= (A_{t-1}\cap P_t)\cup R_t,\nonumber\\
 K_t &= \{k\in\{1,2\}:\forall j\in P_t,\ F_j(k,1)+m\leq d_j\},\nonumber\\
 u_t &= \begin{cases}
 b_t,& A_t=\emptyset,\\
 \max(b_t,\min K_t),& |A_t|>0,\ |K_t|>0,\\
 2,& |A_t|>0,\ K_t=\emptyset.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:guard-update}
\end{align}
The four-replica configuration tests candidates $0,\ldots,4$ and uses fallback four.
Here $d_j$ is the deadline. Triggering includes a one-second lookahead and uses
$\geq$; candidate feasibility uses $\leq$ without that extra second. Empty
$K_t$ with active risk sets an \texttt{infeasible} flag. A latched ID is removed
only when it leaves the pending queue, not when a later estimate becomes safe.
The target never falls below \emph{this tick's} H target. A guard raise can bypass
H's cooldown and writes $u_t$ and the current time back to H's target and
last-change state; it does not guarantee global deadline feasibility.

While $A_t$ is nonempty and both queues have work, dispatch reserves at most
one offline slot. An already-running offline job leaves other slots online-first.
Otherwise, for head-request slacks $s_x=d_x-t-\widehat{p}_x$, online wins
only if $s_{\rm on}\leq m$ and $s_{\rm on}\leq s_{\rm off}$.
Without online backlog, offline dispatch is unrestricted. No running request
is preempted; later scale-out must wait for readiness.

The 26-configuration development search selected H+guard before held-out
input access; its duplicate arm was merged in the formal matrix.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:v3results}
We first trace prediction errors to control actions, then test policy
feasibility and selection, including a prospective HTTP deployment. Transfer
studies probe the boundaries; the guard case explains deadline protection
and its resource--latency trade-off.
\subsection{Cross-level prediction fidelity}

respectively. Figure~\ref{fig:e2cooldown} exposes PPO-2's startup--cooldown interaction: the 60\,s cooldown begins

\subsection{Price sensitivity and external checks}
Repricing fixed event streams over 27 window--GPU--API price cells leaves
recovery and burst savings positive, but steady can become negative
($-39.59\%$); its break-even API multiplier is 1.004. The supplement retains
the full table and scenario assumptions; these are not provider billing forecasts.

External checks cover queue-target (B-HPA), Holt-predictive (B-PRED),
and queueing-based (B-MMC) baselines, Llama, and Azure arrivals
(supplement). Each baseline meets all deadlines in nine runs; H+guard costs less than B-HPA on two
known Qwen windows, with higher API use and queue exposure. Retrospective
replay of 24 Azure runs gives four archived lookup-based models identical 21/24 agreement,
but Lookup-69 falsely accepts three runs while the others reject three feasible runs.
H's physical repeats have mixed outcomes in both windows. Identical
agreement scores thus conceal opposite deployment risks.

\section{Discussion}
A predictor can accept failing PPO deployments yet reject feasible
alternatives in another cohort. The prospective HTTP study validates a
choice frozen before deployment, with every deadline met; its historical-proxy
alternative costs 19.87\% more. Physical validation should check admission
risk and the cost of excluding feasible alternatives.

\subsection{Validating predictions across levels}
Measure setup and scale-out separately; align launch, readiness, draining,
release, targets, and cooldown expiry. In the deployment study, readiness after
expiry permits immediate scale-down where replay predicts protection. Inspect
action trajectories: aggregate resource error can hide temporal misalignment.

The guard uses observed queues and frozen safety estimates to repair H's
held-out recovery failure. H never reads simulator startup parameters;
correcting replay endpoints and guarding physical execution have distinct roles.

\subsection{Resource and latency trade-offs}
Elastic policies exchange online latency margin for lower occupied capacity.
Compare savings under a common feasibility and API/queue/lifecycle ledger.
B-HPA, price, and Llama/Azure checks establish the tested economic and workload
boundaries; meeting one SLO does not establish tighter-SLO suitability.


\paragraph{Practical validation.}
Our screening reversals motivate validating both accepted and rejected
candidates after rule development or PPO training. Freeze the pool and
predictions, then compare physical feasibility and cost under one ledger.
Retain selection ties and report each repeat when physical rankings reverse.
Request-aligned service, action, queue, and resource traces explain failures
that aggregate prediction error alone misses.

\section{Scope and limitations}
Earlier cohorts use a five-second synthetic API timer. The prospective
study generates actual responses on same-host, same-weight HTTP providers
and charges their fixed capacity; it does not establish commercial API
latency, cross-model quality equivalence, or provider billing savings. GPU
release measures occupied resources, not power or energy.

Evidence spans Qwen 7B/14B and Llama 8B, two engines, full-day inputs,
quantization, and up to four same-host replicas. Repeated fixed inputs and
selected days/checkpoints limit population inference. All-feasible cohorts
do not test failure detection. The studies do not establish heterogeneous
scaling, universal rankings, or a general guard guarantee.

\section{Conclusion}
FidelityLoop links replay-based policy selection to physical deadline and
resource outcomes. Startup--cooldown interactions and errors in capacity timing can
admit failing policies or exclude cheaper feasible alternatives despite accurate
service or aggregate-resource estimates. Rule-based and learned policies,
two serving engines, and an external service model exercise the contract;
prospective HTTP execution confirms the feasibility and cost consequence of
a choice frozen before deployment. Traces and CPU tools support reuse.
Elastic-serving replay needs validation
of lifecycle timing, online/offline feasibility, and action trajectories
before deployment.
