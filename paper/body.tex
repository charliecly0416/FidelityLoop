\section{Introduction}
Elastic LLM serving releases idle instances to save resources, but capacity
returns only after startup. Queues, running work, and lifecycle transitions
then shape subsequent control decisions. A simulator used to evaluate such
policies must capture when capacity is available, not just how long individual
requests take or how many GPU seconds a run consumes.

FidelityLoop connects prediction, policy decisions, and physical lifecycle
execution through a shared contract (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}). It tests
whether a replay model preserves the outcomes that matter for policy selection:
deadline feasibility and the resources consumed by feasible executions.
Our measurements reveal two ways aggregate accuracy can mislead this choice.
Startup timing can cross a controller's cooldown boundary and change subsequent
actions; resource errors can also cancel in the total while capacity is available
at the wrong times. Across policies, arrivals, and serving contexts, controlled
physical measurements connect these errors to deployment choices and costs.

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A reusable closed-loop validation framework.} FidelityLoop separates
policy adapters from lifecycle execution and aligns causal observations,
request identities, resource trajectories, and deadline-aware cost ledgers.
Its adapters connect rules and learned policies to vLLM and SGLang, and
substitute Vidur service predictions in replay. Developers can change policies
or backends while retaining comparable evaluation records.
\item \textbf{Mechanisms of replay-to-deployment disagreement.} Held-out physical
runs distinguish service, aggregate-resource, and trajectory fidelity.
Event-level analysis and controlled replay contrasts expose startup--cooldown
interactions and cancellation in aggregate resource error, explaining why
local accuracy need not preserve closed-loop behavior.
\item \textbf{Decision consequences and reproducible evidence.} Retrospective
policy selection connects false accepts and false rejects to failed deployments
and higher costs within tested candidate pools. Rule-based and frozen PPO
policies, additional arrivals, serving-stack changes, and a model-size port
exercise the validation framework across contexts. A guard case study
quantifies deadline protection and resource--latency trade-offs. We release
paired traces and CPU tools for inspecting these outcomes and replaying the
fixed Vidur-backed cases.
\end{enumerate}
The release provides CPU interface checks, a fixed-event cost verifier, and
a paired trace corpus\footnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/FidelityLoop-144B/}};
the supplement documents their reproduction scopes.

\section{Background and motivation}
We consider mixed online and offline LLM workloads served by a limited GPU
pool, where elastic scheduling aims to reduce resource use while meeting
request deadlines. Evaluating every candidate policy on physical hardware
is costly, motivating a framework for replay-based policy development and
training followed by physical validation. FidelityLoop connects these stages
through a shared policy interface and consistent lifecycle and deadline
accounting, so that savings predicted in replay can be checked against
deployment outcomes.

Preliminary measurements reveal the problem: calibration reduced prediction
error for matched local requests while increasing error in occupied GPU time
under dynamic scheduling. These observations precede and motivate the
preregistered held-out study.

The lifecycle diagnosis separated the old 69.219 s startup proxy from the earlier
window launch-to-ready (25.19 s) and setup (46.80 s) contexts. When the proxy
crossed the 60 s cooldown boundary it changed a target decision, motivating the
locked sequence of calibration, lifecycle checks, and held-out feasibility tests.

Figure~\ref{fig:motivation} summarizes this lifecycle boundary; the held-out
selection procedure is fixed separately in Section~\ref{sec:workload}.

\section{Related work}
Vidur and Charon model LLM execution to guide configuration search;
Clockwork targets predictable request timing
\citep{vidur2024,charon2026,gujarati2020clockwork_arxiv}.
FidelityLoop's evaluation unit is an elastic policy rollout: its deadline
classification, resource and control trajectories, and resulting deployment
choice. Its shared policy interface and identity ledger align these outcomes
between replay and physical execution.

Cold-start characterization decomposes initialization latency, while
ServerlessLLM reduces startup overhead
\citep{kabakibo2026coldstart,fu2024serverlessllm}. We study how errors in that
latency change subsequent capacity decisions and deadline outcomes; the replay
endpoint contrast holds the engine and loading path fixed.
vLLM, SGLang, and Sarathi-Serve optimize request execution
\citep{kwon2023vllm,zheng2024sglang,agrawal2024sarathi}; we validate
elastic policies across execution backends and replay service models.

Provisioning and scheduling work connects workload estimates to SLOs
\citep{crankshaw2018inferline,zhang2019mark,zhang2023shepherd}. Our comparisons
evaluate replay models as policy-screening tools: whether their feasibility
and resource predictions support deployment choices. The guard and frozen
PPO checkpoints provide rule-based and learned-policy cases, respectively.

Policy design provides a complementary layer. Llumnix dynamically reschedules
LLM requests \citep{sun2024llumnix}; AAPA predicts scaling needs for serverless
workloads \citep{zhang2025aapa}; KIS-S learns GPU-inference autoscaling with PPO
\citep{zhang2025kiss}. PPO, the standard clipped policy-gradient method
\citep{schulman2017ppo}, provides a learned-policy case for our framework.
We train policies in replay and freeze their checkpoints to test whether
replay-selected policies remain feasible in physical execution.

\section{Problem and metrics}
The system has two accelerators, online arrivals with a 60 s deadline, and 120
offline jobs per 2,100 s observation window with a 300 s deadline. Online requests
run locally or use a delay-only synthetic API sink; offline jobs run locally.
Arrivals span 1,800 s; execution and accounting continue for another 300 s
to cover the remaining deadline horizon.
The primary window cost, in scenario USD, is
\begin{equation}
 C = .001 G + .002 S + .001 D + 10^{-6} I + 3\!\times\!10^{-6} B + .01 M,
\end{equation}
where $G$ is occupied GPU seconds, $S,D$ are starts and shutdowns, $I,B$ are
synthetic API input and output budgets, and $M$ counts offline deadline misses,
including unfinished requests whose deadlines have passed. Setup and cleanup are reported separately and are not folded into the
primary model gate.

Completed means a recorded completion by the observation cutoff; it may be
\emph{on time} (finish time $\leq$ deadline) or \emph{late} (finish time $>$
deadline). An unfinished request has no completed terminal by the cutoff.
Unfinished or censored work is retained in the arrival denominator. The capacity
gate $G_F$ requires all2 (two GPUs throughout) to complete every online and offline
request on time. P1 requires 120/120 offline requests on time, at least 99\%
online requests on time, and no unfinished request; P1+ additionally requires
every online request on time. P2 is a ratio-of-means saving against all1 (one GPU throughout) computed
within each window and then averaged equally across the three windows.

\section{Workload construction}
\label{sec:workload}
Online arrivals in the main study come from the successful-request subset of BurstGPT, a public
trace of production LLM requests \citep{wang2024burstgpt}. We read all 1,404,294
source rows and retain source hashes and row identities. The trace has no
session identifier, so the split separates arrival times rather than sessions.
Locally routed requests execute actual GPU inference with fixed-corpus prompts
and registered token budgets. The experiment reproduces arrival-time patterns,
not the original users' prompts or semantic tasks.

The three held-out windows are selected once from the forward pool after the old
windows plus a 30-minute protection band. A 60 s morphology filter defines the
steady, recovery, and burst-offline classes (even, declining, and bursty online
arrivals); a fixed SHA ordering takes
the first eligible candidate in steady--recovery--burst order, subject to the
registered separation constraint. Online rows are thinned with the inherited
integer threshold, while the 120 offline jobs are constructed at fixed 15 s
spacing and 256-input/256-output token budgets. The two salts and all thresholds
are fixed in \texttt{s4\_generation\_lock.json}; generated inputs are locked before replay
and never replaced to improve a result.

\section{Experimental setup}
The preregistered held-out study used two NVIDIA A100
80GB PCIe GPUs at \experimentfacility{}, with one independent
tensor-parallelism-1 instance per GPU and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. The serving stack
was vLLM 0.9.0 \citep{kwon2023vllm} with PyTorch 2.7.0+cu126, Transformers 4.51.1, tokenizers 0.21.1,
and NumPy 1.26.4. Instances used FP16, maximum model length 4096, maximum batched
tokens 16,384, maximum sequences 1,024, four local request slots, chunked prefill
enabled, prefix caching disabled, eager execution enabled, and GPU memory
utilization 0.8. Readiness required the per-process health probe and the dual-ready
barrier; cleanup required verified process release on both devices. These values are
bound by each run's environment snapshot and GPU identity record.

The deployment-adapted model (Local) was calibrated and frozen before held-out execution. The historical Legacy-refit--Local comparison spans different
hardware environments (A800 and A100), so it does not isolate the
effect of calibration.

The deployment study locks a separate runtime with the same Qwen/vLLM stack:
one all2 anchor and five frozen PPO arms per window, with three repeats per
PPO arm (32 runs). Checkpoint and tensor hashes bind policy identity. An earlier
38-run mixed-policy study covers rules, guards, and PPO under another runtime.
The screening study evaluates six previously undeployed checkpoints on the
same two windows, each on a separate dual-A100 allocation: one all2 anchor
and two repeats per arm (26 runs). These runtime groups remain separate even
where checkpoints are shared.

The engine study transfers six policy arms to SGLang 0.4.8, PyTorch 2.7.1,
and Transformers 4.52.3 on two A100 80GB PCIe GPUs. It retains the Qwen
weights, windows, policies, and accounting contract: two repeats per
arm/window plus two all2 anchors (26 runs). The supplement documents
the full-stack change and token-input compatibility.

The arrival study retains the SGLang/7B stack and frozen predictors on
three additional windows (27 cells, including 24 policy cells). The 14B study ports the
contract to Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct/vLLM on the two locked windows (12 cells),
freezing two predictors that differ only in startup; neither extension retrains
PPO or searches policy parameters.

\section{Method}
Predictive replay and physical execution share a policy contract. Adapters read
observable queues and device states and
return capacity targets; the guard may also select dispatch priority. The backend
handles readiness, draining, and verified release, while request identities align
predictions, decisions, lifecycle states, resources, and costs. A separate CPU verifier recomputes feasibility and cost from raw records. Engine adapters
translate launch, readiness, inference, and release operations into this
contract, allowing a serving backend to change without redefining policy
observations or evaluation metrics.
By default, ready local slots serve online requests before offline jobs.
Online requests still waiting 10 s after arrival can enter the synthetic API
sink, which admits up to eight concurrent requests and completes each on a
5 s timer. The guard can prioritize at-risk offline jobs; PPO changes capacity
while retaining the default dispatch and API rules.

The policy and backend adapters have separate interfaces; the supplement
reports their implementation footprint. CPU checks preserve historical
request/event ledgers, and causal replay reproduces all 29,580 logged
decisions from 16 same-runtime runs. A Vidur service backend
\citep{vidur2024} supplies native scheduling and token execution while
FidelityLoop retains admission, lifecycle transitions, and the frozen guard.
A Qwen2.5-7B/A100 service screen precedes the retrospective policy comparison
in Section~\ref{sec:crosslevel}; the supplement provides profiles and parameters.

and guard parameters; only startup differs (69.219 versus 46.067\,s).

For each model prediction and physical repeat of a window, the dimensionless
lifecycle loss is
\begin{equation}
 L=\frac{0.5|\Delta G_{\rm occupied}|+0.25|\Delta G_{\rm active}|}{2T}
   +\frac{0.25|\Delta Q|}{TN},
 \label{eq:lifecycle-loss}
\end{equation}
Here $G_{\rm occupied}$ sums per-GPU time from launch issuance to
verified release, while $G_{\rm active}$ sums time in the active
state, including idle time; both are measured in GPU seconds.
The difference $\Delta$ is prediction minus observation, $T=2100$ s for formal windows,
and $N$ counts \emph{all} online and offline arrivals. $Q$ is the time integral
of the released-but-undispatched queue, in request seconds. Unfinished requests
remain in $N$ and contribute queue time while queued up to cutoff. Two devices
give the $2T$ normalization. We first average losses over repeats within each
window, then weight windows equally; we do not pool requests or restrict $N$ to
matched-local support. $L$ measures prediction error, separately from scenario
cost $C$; the shorter development horizon is stated with that experiment.

\subsection{PPO policy contract}
The learned policy is trained only in the CPU replay environment, with Lookup-69 or Lookup-30
as the training environment; the physical serving loop is used only after a
checkpoint is frozen. At each one-second decision boundary, its 30-dimensional
causal observation contains normalized time and target state, queue counts and
ages/slack, device lifecycle states and ages, running-job summaries, and the API
queue. The capacity action proposes a target of 0, 1, or 2 GPUs; the registered
60 s cooldown filters that proposal before execution, while the offline action
head is fixed to its sole legal value. The reward is the negative incremental
scenario-ledger cost, so training trades occupied resources, lifecycle events,
API use, and deadline misses under the declared contract.

Training uses the three development arrival regimes and paired seeds from the
locked CPU PPO contract. The five deployment checkpoints are selected mechanically with Lookup-30 replay,
guard disabled, P1+ required in every validation window, and sorting by equal-window
mean cost, checkpoint index, then seed. PPO-1 and PPO-2 were trained with Lookup-69; PPO-3--PPO-5 with Lookup-30.
Checkpoint names denote fixed identities and are reused across studies.
Training settings, data partitions, and checkpoint coordinates are
detailed in the supplement's Frozen PPO training and selection
contract section.

\subsection{Controller and guard}
H uses up-threshold 8, down-threshold 0, and a 60 s cooldown. At each one-second
tick, H reads queue and running counts plus its target/cooldown state, not the
predictor's startup parameter. The guard's lifecycle-delay estimates use separately
frozen safety parameters, distinct from the Lookup-69/Local simulator endpoints.
The guard observes only released queues, device states, and running-job ages;
future arrivals and true completion or readiness times are unavailable. Let $P_t$
be the pending offline request IDs and $b_t$ H's current target after its decision.
The projection $F_j(k,r)$ estimates request $j$'s finish with $k$ devices and
reservation setting $r$, using frozen lifecycle delays, running-job residuals,
and concurrency-four service estimates. It schedules pending jobs by deadline
then request ID over four slots per device. Online backlog precedes offline
work unless reservation ($r=1$) assigns one slot to offline jobs; without online
backlog, offline work can use all slots. The supplement's ``Guard implementation map'' specifies device selection
and overdue-estimate fallbacks. These estimates are not proved completion-time
upper bounds.

For the full guard ($r=1$), margin $m=5$ s and previous risk-ID set $A_{t-1}$,
the update is
\begin{align}
 R_t &= \{j\in P_t:F_j(\max(1,b_t),1)+m+1\geq d_j\},\nonumber\\
 A_t &= (A_{t-1}\cap P_t)\cup R_t,\nonumber\\
 K_t &= \{k\in\{1,2\}:\forall j\in P_t,\ F_j(k,1)+m\leq d_j\},\nonumber\\
 u_t &= \begin{cases}
 b_t,& A_t=\emptyset,\\
 \max(b_t,\min K_t),& |A_t|>0,\ |K_t|>0,\\
 2,& |A_t|>0,\ K_t=\emptyset.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:guard-update}
\end{align}
Here $d_j$ is the deadline. Triggering includes a one-second lookahead and uses
$\geq$; candidate feasibility uses $\leq$ without that extra second. Empty
$K_t$ with active risk sets an \texttt{infeasible} flag. A latched ID is removed
only when it leaves the pending queue, not when a later estimate becomes safe.
The target never falls below \emph{this tick's} H target. A guard raise can bypass
H's cooldown and writes $u_t$ and the current time back to H's target and
last-change state; it does not guarantee global deadline feasibility.

While $A_t$ is nonempty and both queues have work, dispatch reserves at most
one offline slot. An already-running offline job leaves other slots online-first.
Otherwise, for head-request slacks $s_x=d_x-t-\widehat{p}_x$, online wins
only if $s_{\rm on}\leq m$ and $s_{\rm on}\leq s_{\rm off}$.
Without online backlog, offline dispatch is unrestricted. No running request
is preempted; later scale-out must wait for readiness.

The registered development search covered 26 controller configurations,
including guard variants. Selection preceded held-out input access and returned
the preregistered H+guard configuration; its duplicate arm was merged into the
four-arm formal matrix to avoid pseudo-replication.

\subsection{Evidence protocol}
The held-out study evaluates all1, all2, H, and H+guard on steady, recovery,
and burst-offline windows, with three repeats per arm (36 formal runs). CPU verification uses the original acceptance code and lock-bound sources.
Two operator-aborted attempts remain in history but not formal statistics.
Metrics use run and window units. Raw-event recomputation checks terminal
counts, lifecycle occupation, and costs against sealed summaries. A technically valid negative result remains in the matrix.

The fixed gate order starts with full-capacity feasibility ($G_F$), then
tests occupied-resource error, deployment-adapted lifecycle loss,
and matched-request service fidelity. Target agreement is
diagnostic. P1/P1+ and P2 then assess policy feasibility and cost, retaining
H's failures as control evidence. This order separates model validity
from the appeal of a policy's predicted savings.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:v3results}
We first establish the prediction-level mismatch that motivates the framework.
We then test replay feasibility in policy deployments, across new checkpoints,
and across serving contexts. The guard case study connects these validation
results to the resource--latency trade-off.

\subsection{Price sensitivity}
Repricing the same H+guard and all1 event streams over 27 window--GPU--API

\section{Discussion}
FidelityLoop evaluates whether policies meet deadlines and what resources
feasible executions consume. Lookup-30 reduces fixed-H resource error, whereas Lookup-69
matches feasibility better in the mixed-policy study and the deployment study. Tests on new checkpoints,
SGLang, and a 14B vLLM port show why predictions must be revalidated in each
deployment context.

\subsection{Validating predictions across levels}
Lifecycle validation should follow the controller's clock. For controllers
that block scale-down until a target-change cooldown expires, readiness after
expiry may permit immediate scale-down even when replay predicts a service
interval protected from scale-down. The deployment study exhibits this interaction. Comparing deployment startup
timing with controller boundaries is therefore a useful diagnostic; setup
and window scale-out should be measured separately. State and action
trajectories also need inspection because aggregate resource error can
hide temporal misalignment.

The guard shows how a controller can act on deadline risk from observed queues
and frozen safety estimates. It repairs H's held-out recovery failure, but H
does not read the simulator's startup parameters: the endpoint correction and
the guard benefit have distinct causal roles.

\subsection{Resource and latency trade-offs}
Elastic policies trade online latency margin for lower occupied capacity.
The same-runtime comparison with B-HPA quantifies this trade-off in API
share, lifecycle-event counts, and queue exposure. Price sensitivity and
the Llama and Azure tests identify economic and workload boundaries;
meeting one declared SLO does not establish suitability for tighter latency
targets or heavier loads.

The supplementary CPU microbenchmark measures \texttt{adapter.decide} on
reconstructed states, excluding live-serving and full-controller overhead.

\paragraph{Practical validation.}
Measure lifecycle timing for the deployment, then freeze predictions before
physical execution. Check service timing, resource use, and control trajectories
separately; report false accepts and false rejects, retaining each physical
repeat. Condition savings on meeting deadlines. When arrivals or serving
context change, repeat these checks under the same accounting contract.

\section{Scope and limitations}
The synthetic API implements a five-second timer after routing wait, without
answer generation or quality evaluation. Scenario costs thus measure the
declared resource contract rather than equal-quality commercial substitution.
GPU release denotes process and resource release, not physical power-off,
energy consumption, or provider billing.

The studies cover two-GPU deployments, Qwen 7B/14B and Llama 8B, vLLM and
SGLang, and selected trace-derived windows. Repeats measure execution
robustness rather than independent workload coverage. Missing session identities,
constructed offline pressure, and nonrandom checkpoint selection limit
population-level inference. Screening, engine, and 14B studies reuse known
windows; screening and arrival cohorts are all feasible, whereas the engine
and 14B cohorts contain two and four infeasible policy runs.
The evidence does not establish large-scale heterogeneous deployment or a
general ranking of replay models, PPO training environments, or PPO versus
the guard; other model families and quantized inference remain untested.

Runtime identities remain separate: deployment drift confounds Legacy-refit--Local, the screening study uses
one allocation per window, and the engine study changes the engine and dependencies together.
The 14B study isolates startup within the 14B predictor, not the causal effect of model
size, engine, or hardware. The same-runtime baseline comparison includes
dispatch differences, rather than isolating the guard. Guard repairs provide
no general safety guarantee, as the Llama failure illustrates. The supplement
retains technical-attempt history and detailed boundaries.

\section{Conclusion}
FidelityLoop connects replay-based policy selection to physical deadline and
resource outcomes through a shared policy interface and aligned execution
records. Its measurements and replay contrasts show how startup--cooldown
interactions and temporally misplaced capacity can undermine deployment
decisions despite accurate service or aggregate-resource estimates.

Across the tested contexts, replay errors can admit failing policies or
exclude lower-cost feasible alternatives. Rule-based and learned policies,
two serving engines, and an external service model exercise the same
validation framework. The released traces and CPU tools let developers reproduce
these diagnoses and reuse the validation contract.
